The finding, applied to my own work

The model had 47 arguable inputs specified to the cent. An elasticity test shows six of them can change the conclusion and roughly forty cannot, no matter how wrong they are. That alone would be a presentation problem rather than an analytical one.

The real problem is the overlap: the single highest-leverage input carries the weakest evidence. Blended revenue per stall has a DSCR elasticity of 1.67 — more than any other input — and there is no verifiable Hartford-area rate for a secured 53 ft tractor-trailer stall behind it. The number I used was inferred from a national band.

So the honest output was never a DSCR. It is a distribution and a required value: what the rent would have to be, and how likely that is. This document discards what does not survive, reduces what remains to one identity, and sets out a method that generalises to any deal of this shape.

01

Which inputs can actually change the answer

Normalised elasticity of DSCR to each input, by central difference at ±5%. An input with |e| < 0.1 cannot move the conclusion; an input with |e| > 1.0 deserves the entire diligence budget.

Several inputs share an elasticity exactly because they enter multiplicatively. Rate and occupancy both move revenue; acres, yield and SF-per-stall all move stall count. There are not six independent levers — there are three: revenue per stall, stall count, and the financing terms.

02

What I am discarding

Not softened, not caveated — removed from the evidentiary basis. Some of it stays in the workbook as a labelled illustration; none of it should carry weight.

What survives unchanged

The Connecticut assessment ratio (statute), the Bloomfield mill rate (adopted, published), the mortgage-constant arithmetic, the 14.2× cost-to-NOI threshold, and the stall-geometry calculation — which is an engineering derivation anyone can check, not a claim about the world. The zoning finding also stands: it came from meeting minutes, not inference.

03

The reduction — forty inputs are one identity

Everything the workbook computes collapses, exactly, into a single expression. Stating it this way is what made the audit possible: you cannot see that forty inputs are decoration until you can see the six that are not.

DSCR  =  [ N(R − c)·12 − F ]  /  [ LTC · k · (N·v + Φ) ]

N  stalls = A · 43,560 · y / s
R  blended revenue per stall-month
c  variable operating cost per stall-month
F  fixed annual operating cost
v  variable capex per stall
Φ  fixed capex — land, entitlement, infrastructure, financing, reserves
k  annual mortgage constant  ·  LTC  loan to cost
04

Three tests, in order

Rearranged, the identity yields a decision procedure that needs no spreadsheet and applies to any parking or outdoor-storage deal.

Test zero — the sign of leverage

Before any DSCR convention, compare yield on cost to the interest rate. If yield on cost is lower, debt reduces the equity return and the deal is not a financing problem, it is an asset problem.

Test one — is a marginal stall self-financing?

m  =  (R − c)·12  −  D · LTC · k · v
if m ≤ 0, no amount of scale ever works — every added stall deepens the hole

Here m is +$1,647 per stall per year for S2. Scale can work. That is worth knowing before spending a week on site-size analysis.

Test two — is the site big enough for its own fixed costs?

N*  =  B / m   where   B  =  F  +  D · LTC · k · Φ
05

The answer is a distribution, not a number

Monte Carlo over the six identity parameters, with priors deliberately widened to the edge of plausibility — revenue spanning $190–470 per stall-month, occupancy and geometry ranged rather than asserted. 200,000 draws at 10 gross acres.

06

The inversion — state what must be true, then go measure it

Rather than asserting a rent and reporting a DSCR, solve for the rent the site must achieve. This converts an unanswerable modelling question into an answerable market one.

R*  =  c  +  [ F  +  D · LTC · k · (N·v + Φ) ]  /  12N
The question that replaces the model

Can a secured 53 ft stall in Bloomfield be let at $306–387 a month, at 90% occupancy?

That is a market question with a findable answer — a week of calls to operators, brokers and fleet managers within fifteen miles. No amount of further modelling can settle it, and the model should never have implied that it had.

07

The general method

This procedure is not specific to Bloomfield, or to truck parking. It applies to any asset whose economics are “fixed site cost plus a repeated unit” — self-storage, RV and boat storage, outdoor industrial storage, EV charging, small-bay industrial.

  1. Reduce to an identity before building anything. Write the answer as one expression in the fewest terms that reproduce it exactly. If you cannot, you do not yet understand the deal. Validate the identity against the full model — it should agree to floating point.
  2. Rank every input by elasticity, not by intuition. Central difference at ±5%. Inputs below |e| = 0.1 get no further work; inputs above 1.0 get all of it. This takes minutes and reorders the entire project.
  3. Grade every input for evidence, on a scale that has an F on it. A primary source, B published secondary, C derivable arithmetic, D judgement, F invented. Be willing to grade your own work F — six of mine were.
  4. Cross elasticity against grade. High leverage with low evidence is the only cell that matters. Everything else is either safe or irrelevant. That cell is your diligence plan.
  5. Run the sign tests before the magnitude tests. Is leverage positive? Is the marginal unit self-financing? These need no distribution and often end the analysis.
  6. Invert rather than assert. Solve for the value each weak input must take. A required value is falsifiable by a phone call; a predicted value invites false confidence.
  7. Report a distribution and a probability. Where the dominant input is unmeasured, a point estimate is a claim you cannot support. Widen the priors past comfort and report the probability anyway.
  8. Keep a discard list. Write down what you removed and why. It is the only durable protection against the same weak number quietly returning in the next revision.
08

What this changes

The direction of the original conclusion survives — a 10-acre greenfield programme at $175,000 an acre does not work, and scale is the dominant lever. Four things change materially:

Revised recommendation

Stop modelling. Go measure the rent, then look for a large existing paved yard.

The rent survey is the whole deal and it costs a week. If secured 53 ft stalls in the Hartford market clear roughly $300 a month, the search is for 16 acres or more — and a retrofit of an existing paved yard at that size is structurally the strongest configuration available, not the greenfield build the original report centred on. If the rent is materially below $300, no site size in Bloomfield rescues it and the search should move markets.

Grades reflect evidence available to this analysis, not the best evidence obtainable. Several F grades could become A grades with a week of primary research — which is precisely the recommendation.
