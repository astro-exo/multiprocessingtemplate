Finding

None of the three scenarios clears a 1.25× debt service coverage ratio. The best of them covers 0.77×. That is not a marginal miss, and it is not an artifact of one pessimistic input — it survives every reasonable flex of the individual assumptions.

The reason is a single ratio. At 65% loan-to-cost and an 8.67% mortgage constant, a 1.25× DSCR requires a 7.05% yield on cost — which means all-in cost per stall can be no more than about 14.2× NOI per stall. The three scenarios come in at 37.3×, 22.9× and 24.3×.

What does work is scale and density, not price. A 14-acre program at 26 stalls per gross acre clears the test; a 10-acre program at 21.6 stalls per acre does not, at any land price — including free land.

01

The three programs

Bloomfield sits off the interstate — roughly ten minutes to I-91 via Routes 218 and 291 — so this is a home-base and drop-lot market rather than a highway truck stop. Revenue is modeled as 80–85% monthly reserved contracts with the balance sold nightly.

02

Capex, split fixed from variable

The split matters more than the total. Variable costs scale one-for-one with the size of the paved yard, so they set the marginal cost of a stall. Fixed costs are incurred to open the gate at all, and are spread across however many stalls the site yields.

03

Cost per stall

Fixed costs are 56–81% of capital cost in every scenario. That is the structural fact of this asset class, and it is why density and site size — not construction specification — are the strongest levers available.

04

Operations and NOI

Revenue is thin relative to cost, and Connecticut’s operating burden is heavy. Property taxes alone run $37–44 per stall per month at a 34.40 mill rate on the statutory 70% assessment ratio.

The constraint, stated plainly

$309 of revenue per stall per month against $182 of operating cost leaves $127 to carry $34,882 of capital.

That is a 22.9× cost-to-NOI ratio on the best of the three scenarios. Debt at 65% LTC needs it under 14.2×.

05

Coverage

Sized against a 25-year amortization at 7.25%, the annual mortgage constant is 8.674%. Debt is constrained by value long before it is constrained by cost — all three scenarios are worth materially less than they cost to build.

06

What actually changes the answer

Because fixed costs dominate, the strongest lever is spreading them across more stalls. Holding rate at $350 per month and land at $120,000 per acre, site size alone moves the deal from 0.48× to 1.65×.

07

Risks and recommendation

  1. Entitlement is the gating item. Bloomfield’s Town Plan & Zoning Commission has treated outdoor storage as a use that is not permitted as a principal use — approved as an accessory use by special permit, with a Zoning Board of Appeals variance otherwise required. A standalone commercial truck-parking yard likely needs a zoning text amendment or a variance, not just site-plan approval. Budget 12–18 months and structure the land contract as option-and-entitle, never a hard close.
  2. Rate risk is the largest number in the model. The $315–340 per month used here is inferred from the national $150–500 band for full tractor-trailer configurations, not observed from a Bloomfield comp. Hartford-metro marketplace data shows $81–106 per month — but for 12–35 ft spaces, not secured 53 ft stalls. Verify against live asks before committing capital.
  3. Property tax method. Taxes are modeled on an assessor factor applied to cost basis. A Connecticut assessor may instead use the income approach, which at these NOIs produces a materially lower bill. The sensitivity grid in the workbook spans the full range; this is the largest soft spot in the model.
  4. Public supply is a price ceiling. CTDOT is spending $31M to add 180-plus spaces by roughly 2030, taking public capacity from about 420 to 600 — free to drivers. That constrains how far private pricing can run.
  5. Ledge and unsuitable soils are the largest greenfield cost shock in this part of Connecticut, and are covered only by general contingency. Bore before you buy.
  6. Environmental exposure. Connecticut’s Release-Based Remediation program replaced the Transfer Act. The $95,000 carried on the retrofit scenario is an allowance, not a budget.
Recommendation

Do not pursue a 10-acre greenfield build at $175,000 per acre.

Two paths merit real work. Scale up — find 14 or more acres at or below $120,000 per acre with a layout yielding 25-plus stalls per gross acre; that is the only configuration clearing a lender’s test on its own economics. Or buy income rather than build it — an existing paved yard acquired at a basis supporting about $21,000 per stall of all-in cost works where new construction does not, though note the retrofit scenario as modeled fails precisely because $385,000 per acre for improved land is too rich; its residual land value at 1.25× is $42,818 per acre.

Either way the first two dollars should go to a rate survey of secured 53 ft stalls within fifteen miles, and a zoning pre-application meeting with Bloomfield TPZ. Both are cheap, and both can kill the deal before any land is optioned.

Economies of scale in construction

The scenarios above price yard construction as a flat rate per square foot, which hides where the money actually goes. Splitting the section into how each dollar behaves shows why volume buys less than people expect — and what does work instead.

The bound on volume

Tripling the yard from 7 to 20 acres takes 4.9% off construction cost per stall.

Material is 56% of the section and barely tiers; production improves on a flattening learning curve; mobilisation is only 3.3% to begin with. There is no version of this where buying more asphalt makes asphalt cheap.

Specification — changing what gets built

The larger prize is not the paving contract

At 216 stalls the value-engineered scenario carries $21,813 per stall of fixed cost, of which only $8,280 is land. The remaining $13,533 exceeds the entire yard construction bill of $9,338 per stall — and unlike material, most of it does not grow with the site at all.

What does not scale, and what gets worse

  1. Stormwater tracks impervious area almost exactly. Connecticut's industrial stormwater rules require a treatment train plus spill controls, not just detention. A bigger yard buys a bigger basin at the same unit cost. Pervious stall surfacing is the only real relief — a second, usually unmodelled, argument for the hybrid section.
  2. Asphalt haul radius is a constraint, not a volume question. HMA has to be placed hot, so a plant more than about 45 minutes out raises cost at any project size. Confirm the nearest plant before trusting any dollar-per-ton figure here.
  3. Lighting and stall electrical scale linearly with stall count. They are the per-stall discrete items and volume does nothing for them.
  4. Bonding, builder's risk and construction management are percentages of hard cost. They scale linearly by construction and never dilute.
  5. Larger sites cross permitting thresholds. Wider traffic study scope, more CT DEEP attention, longer entitlement. Schedule risk rises with size even as cost per stall falls.
  6. Phasing reverses the mobilisation economy. Each phase re-mobilises every trade, roughly $97,000 a time on this section. Phase for capital or lease-up reasons, never expecting a construction saving.
What to do with this

Specify the hybrid section, then buy site size. In that order.

The hybrid surface is worth $3,988 per stall on its own and costs nothing but a design decision, and it shrinks the stormwater basin as a side effect. Site size is worth far more — $5,257 per stall going from 216 to 600 — but it requires finding and financing the land. Everything else on the list is worth $300 to $900 per stall: real money at 300 stalls, but not what decides this deal.
